# Whole-project review, 2026-11-13

Aaron asked for a step back from everything built: what the game really is, how it is shaping up, how the code is,
how many players it could support as built, what is weak or missing, and what is possible that is not on the table.

How this was done. I read the brief, the roadmap, the hosting note, the two earlier reviews (architecture, 2026-11-03;
pipeline, 2026-11-10), the scorecards and the multiplayer design; counted the code; read `ws.py`, the frame loop in
`main.js` and the parts of `server.py` and `hub.py` that decide scale; checked by search which of the 2026-11-03
findings are still open; and looked at the running game on the main viewer (home screen, the Hoh rain forest as a
deer, the home tile as a raven and as a deer). Section 9 lists what I did not check.

## 1. Verdict

**It is a remarkable world and it is not yet a game.** The idea (your own real place, today's date and weather, with
the people taken out, lived as an animal, with a friend) is original, and the hard part of it exists: one command
builds a tile anywhere on land, and 18 tiles are built. What does not exist is a designed first twenty minutes, a
reason to come back tomorrow, or a single person outside this project who has played it. Breadth has outrun depth,
and it has outrun the engineering that would let strangers in.

The project is 22 days old (first commit 2026-10-23) and has 733 commits. For that age the amount that works is
extraordinary. The same pace is the main risk: each review's fix list has been overtaken by new lanes.

## 2. The game

**What is strong**

- **The premise is felt at the first screen.** The earth with a dot per place and each place's card naming the
  peoples whose land it is reads as a serious, beautiful thing (seen today).
- **The place is real in ways no other game is.** Lidar crowns, streams on the 1 m thalweg, last year's weather hour
  by hour, the Sentinel-2 ground colour by date. The brief's test (a resident finds their own hill from the air) is the
  right test and the home tile is built to pass it.
- **The living systems are deeper than they look.** Senses (sight, scent, sound cues), hunts that run without the
  player, carrion that decays over a real day, tunnels carved in soil, herds of hundreds, a gait engine. Each is a
  real mechanism, not a decoration.
- **The method is honest.** Scorecards say measured, ruled, guessed or blank per layer. Gates refuse a wrong animal
  before more credits are spent. Almost no project says what it does not know; this one does, per tile.

**What is weak**

- **No first experience.** A new player arrives as a spirit, today in fog, with a row of icons and no word on what to
  do or why. The verbs exist (forage, hunt, cache, dig, scavenge) but nothing leads a person into one.
- **No arc.** Nothing persists for the player except acorn caches and dug tunnels. There is no individual life
  (birth, a mate, young, death), no progress, no tomorrow. The needs meters fill and empty.
- **Nothing has been tuned by play.** The roadmap says "first guesses" of the strikes, cooldowns, gait numbers and
  sight chances. Every check so far is mine, through a harness, or Aaron's.
- **Nobody outside has played.** As far as I know the hub holds test accounts only. The brief's two success moments
  (the child and the acorns, the adult and the parking lot) have not been tried on a child or a neighbour.
- **The brief said one tile and beauty first.** Since then the work went to 18 tiles and a world pipeline. That built
  the project's most valuable machine, and it left the home tile unfinished at eye level.

## 4. The code

Size, counted today on main: the client 63,400 lines in 121 modules; the pipeline 35,700 in 83; tools 26,800; the
server, hub and game worlds 3,800; tests 7,800 lines (470 Python tests, all passing on main this morning); docs
24,200 lines, of which the decisions log is 9,800.

**Good**

- The pipeline is the best-engineered part: a fingerprinted step graph, a cost ledger, a licence audit, a species
  registry with fidelity levels, a scorecard, a held-out judge. Rules, not per-species patches.
- The network protocol is careful (quantised deltas, nearest first, a byte cap, ordered events) and privacy holds in
  the code: positions in memory only, logs carry paths only.
- World teardown and graphics-context loss are handled on purpose.
- The server is the standard library only: a 90 MB image that runs anywhere, which suits federation.
- No TODO or FIXME anywhere; open work is written in the docs with its reason.

**Not good**

- **The client has no automated test, no type check, no lint and no CI.** 63,400 lines are verified by hand through a
  browser pane, with harnesses kept in `scratch/` and in agents' notes.
- **A few files hold too much.** `animals.js` is 7,217 lines: simulation, drawing, herds and the network mirror in
  one. `treegen.js` is 3,921. `main.js` has 65 imports (57 at the last review) and a frame function that calls about
  45 systems in a hand-kept order.
- **One exception stops the game.** `frame()` still has no guard (`main.js:1136`).
- **The simulation is tied to the frame.** Animals think on the drawing frame of one player's browser. That blocks
  the server from ever running them (section 5).
- **The 2026-11-03 review's plan was mostly not done.** Fixed: the static file leak. Still open, checked today:
  three.js loaded from unpkg at run time (`client/index.html:355`); sign-in limits keyed on the proxy's address
  (`hub.py:1386`), so one person's failed sign-ins can lock everyone out; trees drawn with culling off
  (`vegetation.js:565`); no GPU timer in the client; no check gate. I found no sign of the other speed steps.
- **The docs are a log, not a map.** The decisions log is 960 KB and changed in 114 of the last 200 commits. It is
  the record of everything and nobody can read it. Aaron cannot review 733 commits; what the project knows lives in
  text that agents write for agents.
- **The work exists on one disk.** Tile rasters, the pipeline cache and the paid Meshy sources are git-ignored; Time
  Machine has no destination configured (`tmutil`, today); the disk had 7.3 GB free this evening (`df`). The Fly volume
  holds a copy of the deployed tiles' client rasters and nothing else.

## 5. Scale: how many players, as built

Nothing above three pages in one world has ever been run (two real browsers; three headless pages in
`tools/mp/visibility.mjs`). Every number below past that is read from the code or estimated.

| Limit | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Players you can see and talk to | about 6 | six wisp slots in `players.js`; the WebRTC mesh (`docs/multiplayer.md`) |
| The shared animals | at its cap with 2 players 600 m apart | one browser streams every animal at 4 Hz under 24 KB/s; measured 2026-11-02 with about 330 animals |
| Sockets | at most 100 pages on the server | 4 sockets a page (counted), Fly's hard limit of 400 connections (`fly.toml`); fewer while tiles download |
| Sign-in memory | about 20 at once | scrypt at 16 MB a hash with no limit on a 512 MB machine (2026-11-03 review, not re-checked) |
| First visit | 15 to 70 MB a tile | `du` of each tile's client folder; served by the Python process, no CDN |
| Who can run it | a recent laptop or desktop | 41 fps on an M1 Pro (2026-11-03), 1 GB heap, no phone tier |

**My estimate: a group of two to six friends in a world works. Past three to five players spread across a tile the
animals away from each player update late, then in jumps. Across all worlds one server should hold 30 to 50
players at once; I would expect trouble before 100.** A public link shared widely would fail at sign-in or at the
tile downloads first.

Two fragile points sit under those numbers:

- **The animals' authority is a player's browser tab.** A hidden tab draws no frames. Nothing hands the host over
  when its tab is hidden (searched: no visibility handling in `animalNet.js`, `animals.js` or `net.js`), so I expect
  every player's animals to stall until that player leaves. Not tested.
- **A host can cheat and a mirror can lie about where it is.** Fine among friends, as the review said.

What the design gets right: worlds share nothing but the hub, so the project scales by adding machines per tile, not
by making one world bigger. A 10 km tile is 100 km2; fifty players in it would rarely meet. The limit that matters
is how many people can be in the same valley and see the same herd, and for that, six to twenty is the right target.

The road up, in order of cost:

1. A load test with headless pages (the pieces are in `tools/mp/`): one day, and every number above becomes
   measured.
2. The real client address, a limit on scrypt, one socket a page, hand the host over when its tab hides: a few days.
   This is what makes 50 safe.
3. Tiles on object storage behind a CDN: removes the download limit.
4. The animals on the server (a fixed-step simulation split from drawing, 4 to 6 days by the earlier review, then a
   headless runner): removes the trust problem and the host's tab, and lets a world hold as many players as the
   stream's budget allows.
5. A realtime service per tile, tiles spread across machines: hundreds to thousands in all, a few dozen per world.

## 6. Weak and missing, ranked

1. **Players.** No one outside has played, and there is no first experience to give them.
2. **Safety before strangers.** The brief's player is a child. The game has open tile chat, voice and video, no
   report button, no moderation, no ban list, and a browser as authority. This must exist before a public link.
3. **Reach.** Desktop GPUs only, a minute to load, no low tier, no phone.
4. **Eye level.** The floor, the understory and the animals' bodies. 469 of 678 species in the registry have nothing
   drawn; 33 animal models are served; three have a hero model; the bobcat was refused twice.
5. **Sound.** Synthesised, and the birds come from the habitat class, not the tile's list. Birdsong is how most
   people know their home by ear.
6. **Whole kingdoms.** No fish on any tile, no sea, no insects.
7. **Licences and the repository.** No LICENSE file; the repository's licences are not chosen. A project meant to be
   built by the people of each place cannot yet take a contribution.
8. **Backups** (section 4).
9. **The engineering floor** (section 4): tests for the client, a guard on the frame, the split of `animals.js`.

## 7. What is possible that is not on the table

- **Traces instead of crowds.** Tracks, scent marks, caches, dens, carcasses and worn trails that persist and that
  other players find. Ten thousand residents playing ten minutes a day never meet live, and would still share a
  world. It costs the server almost nothing and keeps the privacy rule.
- **One life in real time.** You are one acorn woodpecker in one family for one real year. Today's weather is
  today's game; ten minutes a day; the granary is there tomorrow. No other game can match the day outside the
  player's window, and this one already does.
- **Each animal's own senses, drawn.** The brief says each species perceives a slice of the world. Today that is
  mostly who is listed. A deer's two-colour sight, a raptor's magnified centre, an owl's world by sound, a snake's by
  heat, flowers in ultraviolet for a bee: each is a shader, and each is a moment of wonder.
- **Real birdsong, learned by ear.** Recordings per species, season and hour for the tile's own list (each
  recording's licence checked), and a dawn chorus the player learns to tell apart. A child who can name a bird by its
  song outside is the brief's success moment.
- **A bridge to outdoors.** A phone page with no 3D: what is fruiting, flowering and migrating in your tile this
  week, from the calendar the pipeline already builds, and a list of what you have seen for real. This is not a phone
  version of the game; it is the game's reason.
- **Postcards.** Type an address, receive a twenty-second flight over that neighbourhood rewilded, rendered on a
  server. It reaches every phone, needs no GPU from the viewer, and is the way a region's funding drive would spread.
- **Time as a slider.** Before settlement, today, and ten, fifty and two hundred years after people leave, from the
  succession classes the pipeline already reads. With it the pipeline is also a tool for land trusts, watershed
  councils, tribes and schools, who are the natural hosts of nodes and a funding path that fits the mission.
- **Salmon.** A run from the sea to the headwaters across tile seams makes the watershed, the brief's social unit,
  something a player travels. It also starts the missing kingdom.
- **A window.** The world already hunts without the player. An ambient view of your own valley, today, for a
  classroom wall or a second screen, needs no skill and no account.

## 8. What I would do next

1. Freeze breadth for two weeks. One tile (home), one season (now), three bodies.
2. Design the first twenty minutes and put it in front of five neighbours and two children. Watch; change it.
3. In the same two weeks, the floor: a backup, a guard on the frame, a headless smoke test as a merge gate, the load
   test, the four server fixes.
4. Then choose between depth (one life in real time, traces) and reach (postcards, the phone page) with what the
   neighbours showed.

## 9. What this review did not check

- A fair frame rate. The machine was under load; the last fair figure is the 2026-11-03 review's.
- The deployed site beyond its config endpoint.
- Whether the speed steps other than the timer and culling landed in some other form.
- The scrypt cost, taken from the earlier review.
- The hidden-host stall, read from code and not reproduced.
- Recording licences for birdsong, and any cost for server rendering.
- Whether this Mac is backed up some way other than Time Machine.
